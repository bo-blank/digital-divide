You've probably read more AI-generated text this week than you realize. It's in the product descriptions you skimmed, the emails that landed in your inbox, the blog posts that appeared in your search results. It's increasingly in journalism, marketing, and even academic papers.

Most of it passed without notice. Some of it didn't.

What distinguishes the two? And what does it mean to develop fluency in recognizing machine prose in an age when such prose is becoming the ambient hum of our textual environment?

## The Uncanny Valley of Text

There's a reason AI-generated writing often produces a subtle feeling of wrongness, even when we can't immediately articulate what's off. The models that produce this text are trained to predict probable sequences of words—to be plausible rather than true, smooth rather than interesting.

The result is prose that optimizes for average over distinctive. It's text that never takes risks because risk-taking requires a point of view, and a point of view requires having something at stake.

Human writers are full of stakes. We have careers to protect, reputations to build, ideas we genuinely believe. We have editors who will call us out, readers who remember what we wrote last year, a sense of embarrassment that prevents certain sentences from ever leaving our keyboards.

AI has none of this. It has only probabilities.

## The Tells

Over the past two years, as large language models have proliferated, a loose taxonomy of AI writing tics has emerged. Not all AI text exhibits all of these patterns, and sophisticated prompting can minimize many of them. But they appear with enough frequency to be useful markers.

### The Hedging Reflex

AI models are trained to avoid definitive claims that might be wrong. The result is prose saturated with qualifiers:

- "It's worth noting that..."
- "It's important to consider..."
- "This may vary depending on..."
- "While there's no one-size-fits-all answer..."

Human experts *do* hedge when appropriate. But AI hedges reflexively, even when making claims that don't require qualification. The hedging becomes a kind of verbal tic, a way of filling space without committing to meaning.

### The Enthusiasm Mask

AI text often displays a peculiar kind of forced positivity—a cheerfulness disconnected from content:

- "Great question!"
- "Absolutely!"
- "This is a fascinating topic!"
- "I'd be happy to help with that!"

This enthusiasm serves as social lubricant, mimicking the warmth of human interaction. But it tends to appear indiscriminately, applied to requests both mundane and complex, trivial and profound. Real enthusiasm is selective; AI enthusiasm is a default setting.

### The List Compulsion

Ask AI to explain something, and it will often produce a numbered or bulleted list. Sometimes this is appropriate. Often it's a structural crutch—a way of imposing organization on thought that hasn't actually been organized.

The tell isn't the list itself but the quality of what's in it. AI lists frequently exhibit:

- Overlapping categories presented as distinct
- Obvious points elevated to the status of insight
- A relentless parallelism that prioritizes symmetry over substance
- Exactly five items (or three, or seven—the model has learned that these numbers feel authoritative)

### The Thesaurus Effect

AI often produces prose that reads like it was written with one hand on a thesaurus. Synonyms appear in sequences: "crucial, vital, and essential." Ideas get restated with slightly different vocabulary, adding words without adding meaning.

This verbosity stems from the model's training on human text, which often *is* verbose. But human verbosity typically serves a purpose—emphasis, rhythm, the building of an argument. AI verbosity often just fills space.

### The Missing "I"

Perhaps the most fundamental tell is the absence of genuine first-person experience. AI can write "I think" and "I believe," but these phrases are performances rather than reports. There's no actual thinking happening, no beliefs being held.

This becomes apparent when you probe for specificity. Ask a human writer about their opinion, and they can tell you *why* they hold it—what experiences shaped it, what counterarguments they've considered, where their uncertainty lies. Ask AI, and you get a plausible-sounding but ultimately ungrounded response.

## The Deeper Problem

The issue with AI text isn't primarily that it's detectable. Detection will become harder as models improve. The issue is that it's replacing human expression at scale without offering what makes human expression valuable.

Good writing isn't just about conveying information efficiently. It's about:

- **Perspective**: A particular human being seeing the world in a particular way
- **Stakes**: Something genuine at risk in the act of expression
- **Surprise**: Ideas that couldn't have been predicted from what came before
- **Style**: The fingerprint of a mind, accumulated over years of reading and writing

AI can simulate all of these, but simulation isn't the thing itself. A photograph of a meal doesn't nourish you. A description of music isn't music. And text that mimics human writing without human consciousness behind it is, in some fundamental sense, hollow.

## Learning to Read Again

The proliferation of AI text is forcing a kind of re-literacy. We're learning to read not just for content but for authenticity—to ask not just "what does this say?" but "who said this, and why?"

This isn't entirely new. We've always had to evaluate sources, consider biases, distinguish expertise from confidence. But AI adds a new dimension to this challenge: the possibility that there's no "who" at all, just a statistical model optimizing for plausibility.

Perhaps this will make us better readers. Perhaps it will make us more appreciative of genuine human expression when we encounter it. Perhaps it will clarify what we actually value about writing—not just information transfer, but connection, perspective, and the irreducible particularity of individual minds.

Or perhaps we'll simply adjust, accepting AI prose as another form of background noise, no more troubling than stock photography or elevator music.

The outcome depends on whether we decide that the difference matters.
